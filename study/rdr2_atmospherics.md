# Red Dead Redemption 2 atmospherics — reference notes

How Rockstar renders sky, cloud, fog, god rays and the ambient light that falls
out of them, read against what this renderer has. Kept for the same reason as
[`source2_rendering.md`](source2_rendering.md): the interesting part is
architectural. RDR2's whole atmospheric look comes out of **one** system with
one data model, and the reason it holds together is that they refused to build a
second one.

Everything marked **[Bauer19]** is from Fabian Bauer's SIGGRAPH 2019 *Advances in
Real-Time Rendering* talk, "Creating the Atmospheric World of Red Dead
Redemption 2: A Complete and Integrated Solution" — slide text **and speaker
notes**, which carry most of the detail. That is Rockstar's own word.
**[capture]** is imgeself's RenderDoc frame study of the PC build — third party,
observed rather than documented, and it covers the parts of the frame the talk
does not. **[inferred]** is our reading.

> **Why this document exists.** [`source2_rendering.md`](source2_rendering.md)
> §13 concludes that a froxel volume is the shared prerequisite for three
> separate things this project wants — light shafts through blown-open geometry,
> volumetrics inside water, and grenade smoke — and that it is the expensive
> item. Source 2's version of that system is only recoverable in outline, from
> reverse-engineered shaders. **RDR2's is documented in full by the people who
> built it, with formats, resolutions and per-pass costs.** For the one system
> this project has decided it needs and does not have, this is the better
> blueprint. §9 is where it stops being someone else's talk and starts being our
> problem.

## 1. The one thing that matters most

**Clouds, fog, god rays, smoke, interiors and sky ambient are not separate
features in RDR2. They are one participating-medium system, sampled two
different ways.** **[Bauer19]**

The design goals slide is worth quoting almost in full, because every decision
downstream is traceable to it: **[Bauer19]**

> Unified volumetric rendering system — single lighting and data model — cover
> various volumetric materials — cover large and small scale effects —
> seamlessly (!) support interiors and exteriors — **NO POPPING!** — physically
> based — scalable for low and high end platforms.

The two samplers:

| | near | far |
|---|---|---|
| structure | **frustum-aligned voxel grid** (froxels), 160×88×64 | **raymarch**, half res, 1 ray per 2×2 |
| range | dynamic, up to **160 m** | from the last froxel slice to the cloud dome |
| has | fog volumes, local lights, irradiance probes | none of those — dropped for cost |
| stabilised by | temporal filter on material + shadow volumes | 4-frame reconstruction + history clamp |

They **share the lighting and data model**. The same phase function, the same
material parameters, the same visibility term. What changes between them is only
where the samples sit and which features are affordable. **[Bauer19]**

That is the transferable idea, and it is the same shape as Source 2's answer:
the expensive marching happens once, into a volume; every consumer of the result
does one cheap fetch. RDR2 goes considerably further than Source 2 in what it
pushes *through* that volume — see §7, where the volumetric result is fed back
into the ambient probes and the reflection cubemap, so a cloud passing overhead
actually dims the ground.

## 2. Data model — where the medium is

### 2.1 Cloud map

A top-down texture over the world, generated by modulating mask and noise
textures against weather and time-of-day parameters. **[Bauer19]**

| | |
|---|---|
| covers | the cloud dome, **32 × 32 km²** |
| format | **512 × 512, R16G16** |
| R, G | density for cloud layer 1 and layer 2 |

Two channels, two layers, one texture. Note the resolution against the coverage:
512 texels across 32 km is **62 m per texel**. The cloud map decides *where*, and
nothing finer than that — all the shape comes from §2.2 and §2.3.

### 2.2 Cloud height LUT

The cloud map is 2D, so it cannot express cloud *type*. A look-up table of
altitude gradients supplies that — cumulus, altocumulus, stratus. **[Bauer19]**

A dedicated pass copies the slice for the current weather out of a LUT atlas into
a **1 × 128 RGBA8** strip. The copy exists so that weather transitions can blend
between LUTs rather than switching. That is one of the "NO POPPING!" decisions
made concrete: the LUT is copied every frame *specifically* so it can be a blend
target.

### 2.3 Cloud detail

Following Schneider15 (Horizon: Zero Dawn), detail is **carved out of** the low
frequency base shape rather than added to it. **[Bauer19]**

- Two 2D displacement samples, taken at the **xy** and **xz** coordinates of the
  sample position, combined into a quasi-3D displacement vector.
- That vector, plus a wind offset, displaces the lookup into a single **3D
  Perlin/Worley noise** volume.

So: one 3D noise fetch, warped by two 2D fetches. The slide's before/after images
are captioned *no noise → 1×3D noise → 1×3D noise + 2×2D displacement*, and the
displacement is what stops the noise reading as a uniform crust.

The density evaluation, verbatim from the slide: **[Bauer19]**

```c
float2 c        = SampleCloudMap(ray.p);
float3 cloudLut = SampleCloudLUT(altitude);
float  density  = smoothstep(g_CloudShape.xz + cloudLut.xy,
                             g_CloudShape.yw + cloudLut.xy, c.xy);

float rescale(vMin, vMax, v) { return saturate((v - vMin) / (vMax - vMin)); }

density = rescale(noise, CloudLut.z, density);
```

Two things worth pulling out of that last line. `rescale` is inverse-lerp /
`linstep`. Called as `rescale(noise, CloudLut.z, density)` it is
`saturate((density - noise) / (CloudLut.z - noise))` — the noise is the *lower
bound*, which is what makes this erosion rather than modulation, and **the LUT's
z channel controls the softness of that erosion**. The cloud type therefore
controls not just the altitude profile but how hard-edged its detail is, from one
texture.

### 2.4 Rain fog

Fog placed beneath rain clouds, its position derived from the **cloud map's own
density** with a height-based falloff connecting it to the cloud
above. **[Bauer19]** No second authoring pass, no second data source — a
derived effect, which is the unification goal showing up in a small way.

### 2.5 Fog map

The same idea as the cloud map, for ground fog. **[Bauer19]**

| | |
|---|---|
| covers | the **playable** map, **8 × 8 km²** |
| format | **512 × 512, R11G11B10** |
| R | fog start height |
| G | fog falloff distance |
| B | density |

Generated in a composition pass from noise, weather and time-of-day — which is
how morning mist can sit over water bodies at dawn and dissipate as the sun
comes up, without anyone animating it. Blended on top of a global height-based
fog.

Note the asymmetry with the cloud map: 512² over 8 km is **16 m per texel**, four
times finer than the cloud map, because ground fog is seen from inside it.

### 2.6 Fog volumes

Artist-placed, or attached to particles and vehicles. Sphere or box. **Additive
or alpha** blended. **[Bauer19]**

The alpha mode is the clever half, and the notes are explicit about the intent:

- **Additive volumes** add fog at points of interest — hot springs, explosions.
- **Alpha volumes** *replace* the environmental fog with something appropriate to
  an interior.

So a building is authored as: one large additive volume covering the exterior
environmental effect, then interiors **carved out of it** with alpha volumes.
That is how "seamlessly (!) support interiors and exteriors" is delivered without
a lighting-solution switch at the door — the thing the design goals slide put an
exclamation mark on. There is no interior/exterior mode. There is one volume with
regions blended into it.

Up to **32 volumes** are stored in a cluster grid using **Z-binning
[Drobot17]**. **[Bauer19]**

## 3. Shading model

Single scattering, **wavelength-independent** transmittance, with multi
scattering approximated by **two octaves of direct light** following
Wrenninge13 (*Oz: The Great and Volumetric*). **[Bauer19]**

Scattered light at a point is the sum over light sources of luminance modulated
by a phase function `P` and a visibility function `V`. Transmittance is
Beer's law over extinction (absorption + scattering). Nothing surprising; the
interest is entirely in how `P` and `V` are cheated.

### 3.1 Phase function — a stack of HG lobes, then a floor

Bauer is direct that this "took artistic liberties as well as performance
considerations into account". **[Bauer19]**

Henyey-Greenstein alone works for forward-scattering-heavy media like haze, but
**models neither multi scattering nor back scattering well enough for clouds**.
So the phase function is a **composition of HG octaves with the anisotropy `g`
reduced at each iteration**, blended by artist weights *and by the material's
extinction coefficient*.

The structural trick: although the blend happens per sample, **the HG octaves
themselves depend only on ray direction, so they are precomputed once before the
integration**. The per-sample cost is a weighted sum, not a phase evaluation.

They settled on **2 octaves** — diminishing returns past that. **[Bauer19]**

Back scattering is then faked outright, by clamping the phase function up to a
floor derived from the Lambertian BRDF: **[Bauer19]**

```c
float ApplyBackScattering(float phase, float extinction)
{
    float v = 1/PI * rescale(BACK_SCATTER_MIN, BACK_SCATTER_MAX, extinction);
    return max(phase, v);
}
```

Explicitly *not* physically based. The floor scales with extinction, so dense
media get a stronger back-scatter floor than thin ones — which is the right
direction, and about as much physics as it carries. The slide's own comparison
images run *octave 0 → octave 0+1 → octave 0+1+back scattering*, and the third
image is the one where clouds stop looking like grey cotton.

Their tuning values, for calibration: **[Bauer19]**

| material | g |
|---|---|
| cloud | 0.8 |
| high density fog | 0.9 |
| low density fog | 0.9 |
| smoke | 0.1 |

### 3.2 Visibility

Mostly shadow map lookups: **cascade + local lights, terrain shadows, cloud
shadows**. On cloud material *only*, one extra transmittance sample is taken
toward the sun or moon to generate high frequency self-shadowing, at a **distance
controlled by weather parameters** so it can be tuned for large, small or flat
cloud shapes. **[Bauer19]**

One secondary sample. Not a march.

**Terrain shadow map** — raymarch a terrain heightmap along the sun direction,
storing both the intersection height and the ray length: **[Bauer19]**

| | |
|---|---|
| format | **128 × 128, RG16F** |
| R | intersection height |
| G | ray length |

A sample is shadowed by comparing its height against R. **G is used to widen the
comparison threshold** — longer rays get a softer test. That is a one-channel
soft-shadow approximation that costs nothing, and it is the sort of thing worth
stealing on its own merits.

**Cloud shadow map** — an **Exponential Shadow Map [Annen08]** at **768 × 768,
R16F, 6 mips**, blurred down the mip chain to kill aliasing, flickering and
crawling. The depth stored is a **transmittance-weighted** ray length:
**[Bauer19]**

```c
float transmittance = 1;
float depth = maxDepth;
while (ray.t < maxDepth && transmittance > 0.001)
{
    float density = SampleCloudDensity(ray.p + ray.d * ray.t);
    transmittance *= exp(-density * stepLength);
    depth = lerp(depth, ray.t, pow(transmittance, 4));
    ray.t += stepLength;
}
```

`pow(transmittance, 4)` biases the recorded depth hard toward where the cloud
first became opaque. A volume does not have a depth; this picks the most
defensible one.

### 3.3 Ambient

Two sources, one per sampler, and the split is a pure cost decision: **[Bauer19]**

- **Raymarch** — an average sky ambient *without* clouds. Sky scattering is
  rendered into a **low resolution paraboloid** texture and **parallel-reduced**;
  samples are weighted toward the sun/moon direction. Visibility reuses the same
  secondary directional sample from §3.2 rather than gathering in all directions.
- **Frustum volume** — samples the **irradiance probe field**, which carries sky
  light *plus* indirect bounce light, modulated by large-scale AO.

The comparison slide runs *no ambient light → sky average → irradiance probes +
AO*, and the third is dramatically better indoors. Which is exactly the argument
[`source2_rendering.md`](source2_rendering.md) §11 makes for a probe grid, from a
different direction.

### 3.4 Local lights, lightning, rainbows

- **Local lights** — sampled from the light cluster volume, visibility from
  shadow maps with no extra tracing, and **a single HG octave** rather than
  two, for cost. **[Bauer19]**
- **Lightning** — brute force. Each strike pushes a point light into an array;
  every sample step loops the array, applying exponential falloff and the HG
  phase, with **local extinction standing in for shadowing** — which the notes
  say is what gives the lit region shape rather than a uniform glow. **[Bauer19]**
- **Rainbows** — not artist placed and not physically based. Inner and outer bows
  are derived from view and sun/moon direction and treated as **an additional
  light source in the scattered-light sum**, with intensity driven by the *water
  droplet density* material parameter. **[Bauer19]**

That last one is the unified data model earning its keep: a rainbow is not an
effect, it is a light source, and its strength is a channel in the material
volume.

## 4. The frustum volume — three volumes, not one

Based on Wronski14 and Hillaire15. Dynamic depth coverage **under 160 m**, which
artists can *reduce* in interiors to fight undersampling. Resolution
**160 × 88 × 64**. Split into three clip-space volumes. **[Bauer19]**

### 4.1 Shadow volume — `R16F`

The directional light shadow term, gathered from the cascade, the cloud shadow
map and the terrain shadow map. **Temporally filtered**, to resolve undersampling
on high frequency shadow detail. **[Bauer19]**

Separating this out is the non-obvious move. Shadow lookups are the expensive
part of the sample, they are the part that aliases, and they are the part that
survives temporal filtering cleanly — so they get their own volume and their own
filter.

### 4.2 Material volume — 2 × `RGBA8UB`

Eight material parameters across two sub-volumes: **[Bauer19]**

| volume A | | volume B | |
|---|---|---|---|
| R G B | scattering coefficient | R | phase anisotropy `g` |
| A | absorption coefficient | G | global emissive intensity |
| | | B | ambient intensity |
| | | A | water droplet density |

Two notes from the speaker notes worth keeping. **Emissive fog is not per
volume** — there is a single global emissive light value, and this parameter
scales it per volume. And **ambient intensity is mainly an interior tool**, used
as a means of applying ambient occlusion to the medium.

**The accumulation pass** samples and blends every material source — global fog,
clouds, fog map, fog volumes — each with its own tiling noise lookup animated by
wind. The blend order is stated explicitly and it matters: **[Bauer19]**

> **additive ⇒ alpha ⇒ particles**

Additive first so environmental effects are laid down; alpha second so interiors
carve themselves out of that; particles last so fires, explosions and a
smoke-filled saloon vary density inside whatever they land in.

**Temporal filtering, and the leak it causes.** Volume A only. The reprojection
motion vector is **wind velocity**, since that is what the noise lookups are
animated by. But that reprojects an interior voxel to where the *wind* would have
carried it, which may be outside — so exterior fog leaks in. The fix is a fourth,
small volume: **a reprojection velocity magnitude volume, `R8UB`**, which scales
the motion vector down by the opacity of alpha and particle volumes. **[Bauer19]**

That is a very specific bug with a very specific fix, and it is the kind of thing
that only shows up in a talk by someone who shipped it.

### 4.3 Scattered light volume — `RGBA16F`

Samples the material and shadow volumes, accumulates direct light (sun/moon and
local lights) and ambient light (irradiance probes). RGB is scattered light, A is
extinction. A **slice march** then integrates in-scattering and transmittance
along the volume, producing the accumulation volume that deferred and forward
passes look up. **[Bauer19]**

**No temporal blending here, deliberately.** Two named reasons: **[Bauer19]**

1. Ghosting on moving lights.
2. **Trailing on *static* lights in highly anisotropic fog when the camera
   moves** — because strongly forward-scattering media are heavily view-vector
   dependent, so the correct result changes with camera motion even when nothing
   in the world has.

Instead: **dithered lookups of the accumulation volume, plus TAA.**

Reason 2 is the subtle one and it generalises past fog. Any view-dependent term
is wrong to reproject, because reprojection assumes the shaded value belongs to
the surface rather than to the ray. Flagging it here because §9.3 says this is
precisely where our own setup diverges from RDR2's.

## 5. The raymarch, and the reconstruction that makes it affordable

Froxel volumes undersample at distance — limited resolution in every dimension,
exponential slice distribution — and are unstable there even with strong temporal
integration. So a second sampler takes over. It is more memory-efficient but the
loop is expensive, so it **drops fog volumes, irradiance probes and local
lights**. **[Bauer19]**

**Ray length** is bounded by the depth buffer and by analytic intersections with
the ground plane and cloud dome. **Ray origin** sits just past the last froxel
slice, offset by blue noise. Target resolution is **half screen**. **[Bauer19]**

**Step refinement**, after Schneider15: on hitting a cloud's base shape, halve
the step length and step *back* by that amount; repeat for a fixed `i_max`
iterations (the slide diagrams `i_max = 3`) to land close to the true entry
point, then integrate at the reduced step. Return to the base step on leaving the
shape. Bauer notes the honest failure mode — **a thin cloud can be stepped over
entirely**, so base step length has to be tuned against the cloudscape data and
LUTs. **[Bauer19]**

### 5.1 One ray in four, reconstructed over four frames

Even at half res, one ray per pixel was too expensive. So: **cast 1 ray per 2×2
half-res tile, reproject the other three from history.** **[Bauer19]**

History validation is a **colour AABB clamp over the 3×3 neighbourhood** of
in-scattering and transmittance — with a refinement that matters: **neighbourhood
pixels whose depth differs strongly from the current pixel are excluded from the
AABB**. Including them inflates the box until it cannot clamp anything, which is
what produces ghosting in motion. Where no history exists (frustum edges), the
nearest depth sample from the neighbourhood is used instead.

### 5.2 Ray placement — three attempts, and why the first two failed

This is the most instructive part of the talk, because it is a full account of
getting something wrong twice. The goal: **place rays so the surviving
neighbourhood represents the local depth situation**, or the clamp above is
clamping against a lie. **[Bauer19]**

Depth is first downsampled full → half in a **checkerboarded min/max** fashion.
Then:

| attempt | rule | why it failed |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | uniform ray position from the frame index (0–3) | a tile can end up with **only long rays**, so nothing in the neighbourhood carries up-to-date near depth — the clamp goes overly aggressive and the result flickers |
| 2 | frame index **+1 offset in a checkerboard pattern** | covers the full depth range in the example — but still fails in isolated cases |
| 3 | checkerboard offset **plus depth neighbourhood analysis** | **shipped** |

Attempt 3: compare the depth min/max of the current 2×2 tile against those of the
eight neighbouring tiles. **If all neighbours sampled high depth, choose an index
with low depth, and vice versa** — place the ray at the under-represented depth.

The reason attempt 1 is worth recording is that it is the obvious implementation.
A rotating 4-frame index is what anyone writes first, and it is subtly wrong in a
way that shows up as flicker, not as an obviously wrong image.

### 5.3 Upscale

Half → full res by a **dithered upscale with blue noise offsets**
[Stachowiak15][Gjoel16]: four taps, averaged, **or the closest-depth tap on a
depth discrepancy**. **The kernel width is chosen from the transmittance-weighted
ray depth of the pixel** — narrow at distance, so far detail is preserved rather
than blurred away. The same principle governs lookups into the accumulation
volume. **[Bauer19]**

## 6. Scene integration

### 6.1 Sky scattering

Precomputed Atmospheric Scattering [Bruneton07/08] with the improvements from
Hillaire16 — including the **ozone** term. RDR2 additionally **supports earth
shadow**, which Bauer calls out as important for plausible open-world rendering
under a dynamic time of day. LUTs are updated by **time slicing**, and the result
is cached into **yet another frustum-aligned volume, 32 × 16 × 32**, serving as a
LUT for both forward and deferred lighting. **[Bauer19]**

### 6.2 God rays, for almost nothing

Sampling sky scattering at every marched sample would be correct and is too
expensive. Instead it is **applied once, at the transmittance-weighted depth**
(after Hillaire16). **[Bauer19]**

Which loses light shafts — so **the visibility term is separated out of the sky
scattering integration**:

1. Every directional-light visibility lookup along the ray is accumulated,
   weighted by the area the sample covers **and by the transmittance along the
   ray**.
2. On ray termination, normalise by total ray length.
3. Modulate the sky scattering term by that value.

Bauer: *"not physically correct but gives plausible results for very little added
cost."* **[Bauer19]**

**This is the single highest value-per-line idea in the talk [inferred].** It
buys god rays out of a shadow term that is already being computed, without a
second march and without per-sample sky scattering. The composition slide splits
it into *weighted depth* and *shadow area* before the final image, and the shadow
area image is essentially a free god-ray mask.

### 6.3 Final composition

After opaques, before alphas, the deferred application merges: **accumulation
volume lookup + raymarch result + sky scattering and transmittance**. **[Bauer19]**

Plus a **cirrus layer at 8 km altitude** — a *single 2D texture lookup* for
density, but running **the same shading model as everything else**. The
unification again: the cheapest possible cloud representation still goes through
the same phase function and the same visibility.

### 6.4 Sky irradiance probes — the volumetrics feed the GI

This is where RDR2 goes past Source 2, and it is the part worth arguing
about. **[Bauer19]**

| | |
|---|---|
| grid | **32 × 32** probes |
| footprint | **256 × 256 m² per probe** |
| encoding | **3rd order spherical harmonics** |
| placement | ordered grid; height and directionality from a height map and a **bent normal** map, prebaked |

Each probe casts rays — constrained by occlusion and surface gradient — into the
world, **accumulating in-scattering and transmittance**, and applies the result
to the sky Rayleigh/Mie term in that direction (held as a low-res paraboloid).

The consequence: **blue-sky area lighting on a clear day, and indirect light from
cloud bodies when overcast.** Bauer points at the bright patches of indirect
light breaking through the cloud layer onto the ground, and at cloud/mountain
contact shadows. A cloud passing overhead genuinely darkens the terrain beneath
it, because the ambient probes traced through it.

That grid is then the **basis for a higher resolution probe field**, optimised
by: **[Bauer19]**

- A **classification pre-pass** computing main-viewport visibility, driving
  indirect dispatches.
- **32 rays if visible, 16 if not.** Invisible probes cannot be skipped, because
  reflections need valid data.
- One ray per thread, arranged in a **Hammersley hemisphere**, then **parallel
  reduction in shared memory** to build the SH coefficients.
- A **simplified raymarch**: no high frequency noise erosion, no rainbows or
  lightning, larger steps, no refinement.

**0.22 ms on PS4.** **[Bauer19]**

### 6.5 Directional light, reflections, water

- **Directional light** — no additional rays; cloud shadowing on the world comes
  from the cloud shadow map of §3.2. **[Bauer19]**
- **Reflection probe** — the camera-position cube map is responsible for the
  majority of reflections, so a **low-res cube map of in-scattering and
  transmittance** is generated with the simplified raymarch and composited in the
  deferred reflection pass. The slide's warning is *"needs to match sky
  irradiance probes!"* **0.15 ms on PS4.** **[Bauer19]**
- **Water reflections** — a mix of SSR, reflection probe, planar reflections and
  height map tracing. **[Bauer19]**

The water detail is a correctness trap worth transcribing exactly: **SSR is
sampled without alpha effects and without volumetrics**, because the SSR result
*already contains* in-scattering and transmittance for the ray from camera to
reflected depth — applying the volumetric term again would double it. A
**dedicated raymarch pass** supplies the volumetric term for the reflected
direction instead: simplified, heavily temporally blended (running exponential
average), at **512 × 256**, **0.19 ms on PS4**. **[Bauer19]**

The payoff, per the slide: correct reflection angles, no screen-edge
discrepancies, and clouds actually appear in the water — the captioned failure
case is *"missing cloud reflections"* under plain SSR. Read this alongside
[`sea_of_thieves_water.md`](sea_of_thieves_water.md) and
[`source2_rendering.md`](source2_rendering.md) §12.2: CS2's water shader has the
three-tier SSR-over-cubemap ladder but nothing that puts *volumetrics* into the
reflection, and this is the missing piece.

## 7. Performance

The full atmospheric budget, transcribed from the performance slide's label and
value lists in slide order, **measured on PS4**, in a **30 fps** game: **[Bauer19]**

| pass | cost |
|---|---|
| Cloud / Fog Map | 0.040 ms |
| Cloud ESM | 0.156 ms |
| Shadow Volume | 0.204 ms |
| Material Volume | 0.330 ms |
| Scattering Volume | 0.340 ms |
| **Raymarch** | **1.310 ms** |
| Raymarch Reconstruction | 0.232 ms |
| **core total** | **≈ 2.61 ms** |

Plus, from their own slides: sky irradiance probes **0.22 ms**, reflection probe
**0.15 ms**, water reflection raymarch **0.19 ms** — call it **≈ 3.17 ms**, or
roughly **10% of a 33 ms frame**, for the entire atmospheric world.

Bauer's own performance notes: **[Bauer19]**

- Heavily dependent on cloud cover and fog composition — *"artists responsible
  too!"*
- **Worst offender: long rays.** Because it is a shader loop, cost tracks ray
  length and iteration count directly, and **rays terminate at different times**,
  freeing wavefronts in stages and dropping utilisation until the last ray
  finishes.
- **Fully compute-based**, which is what allowed dispatches from the raymarch and
  the frustum volume to be **overlapped**, and some passes to run on **async
  compute**.
- **Time slicing**: only **1 ray in a 4×4 tile** is updated for the cloud shadow
  map per frame, and the terrain shadow map is temporally refined — justified by
  directional shadows not changing rapidly in normal gameplay.

**The raymarch is half the budget**, and it is the half that exists only because
the world is 32 km across. Hold that thought for §9.

### Future work they named

More material variety; **wavelength-dependent transmittance**; **volumetric
shadows**; localized weather; improved denoising. **[Bauer19]** The first two are
the acknowledged simplifications in §3 — transmittance is wavelength independent
today, and visibility never marches the medium.

## 8. What the rest of the frame looks like — [capture]

From imgeself's RenderDoc study of the PC build. Third party and observed, so
treat channel assignments as read off a capture rather than documented; included
because the talk covers only the atmospherics.

**Pass order** — mud/displacement → sky and cloud compute → environment map
generation → GBuffer (with a terrain depth prepass) → shadow maps → lighting
(global directional, then local deferred) → water and reflections → forward
translucent → post.

Note that **sky and cloud compute runs before the GBuffer**, and environment map
generation after it — consistent with §6.4/§6.5, where the atmospheric result is
an *input* to ambient and reflections rather than something layered on at the
end.

**GBuffer** — deferred, seven targets:

| target | format | contents |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | `RGBA8_SRGB` | albedo; A used at the AA stage |
| 1 | `RGBA8_UNORM` | normals; A cloth/hair related |
| 2 | `RGBA8_UNORM` | R reflectance (f0), G smoothness, B metallic, A shadow mask |
| 3 | `RGBA8_UNORM` | R cavity, A hair, rest unidentified |
| 4 | `RG16_FLOAT` | screen-space velocity |
| 5 | `D32S8` | reversed-Z depth; stencil tags mesh groups |
| 6 | `RGBA8_UNORM` | R baked AO, rest unidentified |

Worth noting **R reflectance separate from B metallic** in GBuffer 2 — the same
decision Valve documents for Source 2, where a greyscale reflectance map keeps
metalness from driving the specular *amount*
([`source2_rendering.md`](source2_rendering.md) §3). Two unrelated engines, same
call.

**Shadows** — directional CSM, 4 cascades, **1024 × 4096 `R16_UNORM` atlas** on
medium (1024² per cascade). Spot and point arrays at three quality tiers,
**512×768 / 1024×1536 / 2048×3072, D16**.

**Environment maps** — dynamic per-frame cubemaps built from a **thin GBuffer**
(albedo `RGBA8_SRGB`, normal `RGBA8_UNORM`, depth `D32S8`, baked AO `R8_UNORM`,
final `R11G11B10_FLOAT`), static opaque geometry only, frustum culled per face,
at reduced LOD. Baked interior cubemaps ship compressed (`BC3`). **Split-sum
approximation** for IBL.

**Bloom** — `R11G11B10_FLOAT`, **7 mips**, **non-thresholded input**, 13-tap
bilinear downsample and 3×3 tent upsample (i.e. the Jimenez/COD chain), combined
through an `R8_UNORM` **bloom intensity mask** that rises with distance.

The non-thresholded input is the notable one. Cross-reference
[`source2_rendering.md`](source2_rendering.md) §10.5: our `GlowPass` is already a
good octave-summed chain, it just runs on the backbuffer after tonemap. Moving it
to the HDR target ahead of the tonemap blit puts it in the same shape as this.

**Mud displacement** — **2048² `R16_UNORM`**, footprints and wagon trails applied
by parallax occlusion mapping. Not atmospheric, but it is the same pattern as
Source 2's persistent authored-space masks
([`source2_rendering.md`](source2_rendering.md) §12.1) and the same pattern
destruction scorch would want here.

## 9. What this means for this renderer

This is where it stops being a talk about a 32 km open world.

### 9.1 The honest framing

RDR2 is a 30 fps, compute-heavy, deferred, open-world console renderer with a
dedicated graphics team and a 3.2 ms atmospheric budget. This is a forward
raylib/GL 3.3 renderer for a bounded 24 × 24 tactical board. Most of the talk is
not applicable, and pretending otherwise would waste the parts that are.

What *is* applicable is specific: **we have already decided we need a froxel
volume** ([`source2_rendering.md`](source2_rendering.md) §10.4, §13), for three
independent reasons, and this is the most complete public description of one.

### 9.2 Our world fits inside the near sampler

**The single most useful observation here [inferred].** RDR2's froxel volume
covers up to **160 m**. This project's board is **24 × 24 tiles**, and
[`Constants.hpp`](../src/core/lattice/Constants.hpp) puts a tile at 96uu ≈
**1.5 m** — so the entire playable world is roughly **36 m across** and, at
`kCellHeight` = ⅔ tile over 9 storeys, about **9 m tall**. The whole lattice fits
inside a single froxel volume several times over, with room for the camera to
pull well back.

Consequences, and they are all good:

- **The raymarch is unnecessary.** No far distance to cover. That deletes the
  most expensive pass (1.31 ms of 2.61 ms), the 4-frame reconstruction, the
  colour AABB clamp, the depth-aware neighbourhood rejection, and all three
  attempts at ray placement in §5.2. Roughly half the talk, and much more than
  half of its difficulty, is about a problem we do not have.
- **The cloud map, the cloud LUT, the cloud detail displacement, the cloud ESM,
  the terrain shadow map and the cirrus layer are all out of scope too.** Those
  serve a dynamic-weather 32 km sky. We have an analytic sky and a tactical
  camera looking down.
- **The froxel resolution can be far coarser.** 160×88×64 covers 160 m with an
  exponential slice distribution. Over ~58 m of world, a much smaller volume
  gives comparable or better world-space density — and the `sqrt()` depth
  distribution that Source 2 uses (`source2_rendering.md` §13) matters less when
  the depth range is this short.

What remains is the genuinely useful core: **material volume → shadow volume →
scattered light volume → slice-march accumulation → one fetch per shaded pixel**,
plus the phase function, plus §6.2's god rays.

### 9.3 Three places RDR2's answer does not transfer

**1. It is compute-based, and we are not.** Every shader in
[`assets/shaders/`](../assets/shaders/) is `#version 330`, and there is no
compute path. Compute shaders are GL 4.3. RDR2's entire structure — three
volumes written by dispatches, parallel reduction in shared memory, overlapped
and async dispatches — assumes compute.

The blocker is smaller than it looks. raylib's build already offers
`OPENGL_VERSION` values including **4.3**, and `rlgl` already exposes
`rlLoadComputeShaderProgram` (it warns *"Compute shaders not enabled. Define
GRAPHICS_API_OPENGL_43"* precisely because the path exists). Our build currently
leaves `OPENGL_VERSION=OFF`, which takes the 3.3 default. So this is a build-flag
decision with a hardware-support consequence, not a rewrite — **but it should be
taken deliberately, before anything is designed around it**, because the
alternative (writing 3D volume slices via layered FBO rendering) shapes the code
very differently. **[inferred]**

**2. We have no TAA, deliberately, and RDR2's stability plan depends on it.**
§4.3 is explicit: no temporal blending on the scattered light volume, because
view-dependent scattering trails when the camera moves — the stability comes from
**dithered lookups plus TAA**. This project chose 2× supersampling over TAA
([`source2_rendering.md`](source2_rendering.md) §8) and has no motion vectors.

So the dither has nothing downstream to resolve it. Options, unresolved
**[inferred]**: rely on 2× SSAA to partly resolve the dither (it is a real
resolve, just a weaker one); use a finer volume since we can afford one at this
world size; or blue-noise dither with a fixed per-pixel pattern rather than a
temporally cycling one, trading noise-in-motion for stable noise. **This is the
open question in adopting the architecture and it should be settled by
experiment, not by argument.**

Worth noting the camera helps: a turn-based tactical camera is stationary most of
the time, which is the best case for temporal methods and the worst case for
detecting their absence during development. Test while orbiting.

**3. Their material volume is `RGBA8UB` × 2 because they have eight parameters.**
We would have perhaps three at the outset — scattering, absorption, `g`. Do not
copy the packing; copy the *split* (material separate from shadow separate from
scattered light), which is what makes the temporal filtering tractable.

### 9.4 What to take, in order

1. **The three-volume split, and the accumulation pass.** §4. This is the
   architecture, and it is the same shape Source 2 uses, described in far more
   detail. One 3D fetch per shaded pixel, premultiplied in-scatter in RGB and
   transmittance in A.
2. **The blend order — additive ⇒ alpha ⇒ particles — and interiors as alpha
   volumes carved out of exterior fog.** §2.6. This project has buildings on a
   destructible board and a camera that goes inside them; it is the exact problem
   the alpha volume solves, and it solves it without an interior/exterior mode
   switch.
3. **§6.2's god rays.** Accumulate the directional visibility term you are
   already computing, weight it by sample area and transmittance, normalise over
   ray length, modulate the sky term. Light shafts through a hole a grenade just
   made is a named target in
   [`source2_rendering.md`](source2_rendering.md) §10.4, and this is the cheapest
   credible route to it.
4. **The phase function.** Two HG octaves with decreasing `g`, precomputed per
   ray direction, blended by artist weight and extinction, floored by
   `max(phase, 1/PI * rescale(...))` for back scattering. Their `g` values in
   §3.1 are a calibration starting point. Smoke at `g = 0.1` is directly the
   grenade smoke case.
5. **Volumetrics into the ambient probes.** §6.4. This is the coupling
   [`source2_rendering.md`](source2_rendering.md) §11 does not currently plan
   for: our probe grid is specified to gather sky and bounce, and RDR2's traces
   **through the medium**, so smoke in a room dims what the room's probes gather.
   Given that our probe rays are `RayCaster` rays over a lattice that will
   already know where smoke is (source2 §14), the marginal cost is small and the
   payoff — a smoke cloud actually darkening the space under it — is large.
   **[inferred]**
6. **The water reflection double-counting fix.** §6.5. Cheap to get right up
   front, painful to diagnose later.

**Not worth taking:** the raymarch and everything serving it (§5), the cloud data
model (§2.1–2.4), the cirrus layer, the terrain shadow map, ESM cloud shadows,
rainbows, lightning. All are answers to open-world problems.

### 9.5 Where this sits against the existing plan

[`source2_rendering.md`](source2_rendering.md) §10 ranks froxel fog **fourth**,
behind indirect light, local lights and environment cubemaps. Nothing here
changes that ordering, and two things reinforce it:

- **§6.4 makes the probe grid a prerequisite, not just a priority.** RDR2's
  froxel volume samples the irradiance probe field for its ambient term; the
  raymarch falls back to a crude sky average precisely *because* it cannot afford
  the probes. If we build the volume before the probe grid, we get the raymarch's
  ambient quality with the volume's cost. Build §11 first.
- **§3.4 makes local lights a prerequisite too.** Light shafts need lights.
  A volumetric system with one directional light produces god rays and fog, and
  none of the interior atmosphere that is most of what it is for.

The one adjustment worth making to that plan: **§9.3's compute decision belongs
much earlier than the fog work itself**, because a realtime probe grid, screen
space GI, and any froxel volume all want compute, and the flag is cheap to flip
now and expensive to design around later.
