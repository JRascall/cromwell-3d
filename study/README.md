| [`ruse.md`](ruse.md) | How **R.U.S.E.** and Eugen's **IRISZOOM** work, **read from the retail install on this machine** rather than from the outside — `edat` archives, `EUG0/CNDF` object files and marshalled Python 2.5, opened with three readers kept in §11. This is the note [`map_scale.md`](map_scale.md) said should be written if Eugen ever published; they did not, but they shipped their whole content pipeline as data. **§1.2 corrects the older note's central number**: R.U.S.E.'s largest map is **10.3 km²**, not 150, and `PopCapTotal` is **200 units across every player** — so this is not an extent problem or a count problem but a *view* problem, and every decision below follows from the camera being allowed anywhere from 20 m up to the whole map with no loading. Terrain is **two independently baked meshes, not one LOD chain** — high-def in 327.68 m chunks, low-def in 655.36 m, verified across all 33 maps because chunk count × 327.68 m reproduces the measured extent in both axes every time — with their own separate world bounding boxes because vertices are quantised per chunk. §3.2's `TTerrainMultiScaleBuilder_LevelDescriptor` carries the streaming priorities, and they **cross over**: a coarse tile that is merely *near* outranks a fine tile that is *in view*, which is what a camera that zooms out faster than a disc can fill actually needs. **§5 is the answer to "how do they handle grids": they do not.** Line of sight, camera collision and territorial zones all go to `TStreamedMeshKdTree` — streamed, compressed, subtree-paged — and there are **three trees per map split by *purpose*, not contents**: terrain-only (102k triangles), objects-only (**556 triangles**, one subtree, 6 KB — villages and forests are not in it, because forest is a stat), and a camera tree extending 175 m past the map edge. The occlusion triangle count is a **budget, not a resolution** — a 6× larger map gets the same tree — so LOS costs the same everywhere. The **ruse zones are the same class**: an arbitrary polygon triangulated and extruded, so the whole territorial partition of a 2 km map is 686 triangles and nine kilobytes. Also: a **data-declared render graph of 98 `TRenderLayer` objects in 2010**; Cg cross-compiled to **1,806 precompiled shaders deduplicated by hashing the source *after* generic-function substitution**; impostors that carry their own sun-view matrix and depth shadow; the **`div_map`** whose identical twin `Div_map.png` is still required by WARNO's 2024 map pipeline; a 214-property `TTunableConstante` quoted at length in §7.1 (with the shipped unit inconsistency it produced); order **hold times declared per zoom level** — the hook built to differ and shipped tuned flat; an AI that is a *production-request economy* whose attack trigger reads the **same force-ratio evaluator the player's danger indicator reads**, which is why Le Dressay can say it does not cheat; and **an AI regression suite in the retail build** — a 27 KB flat map, six scripted factions and 21 assertions, §10.3's recommendation. **§11 is the twelve-year diff** — *Wargame: Red Dragon* (2014) read as a version-2 `edat` archive and *WARNO* (2022) read as Eugen's own **plain-text NDF source**, which their mod pipeline exports with comments intact. **652 of R.U.S.E.'s 1,091 classes survive verbatim into Red Dragon**, the kd-tree among them — so §5's answer is not a small-map quirk. What was rebuilt is almost entirely one move repeated: **a special case became a typed table and the behaviour became a mask over it.** Forest-the-constant (`CouvertBonusForet = 78000`) became `TGameplayTerrain` × 22 — with `BloqueVision` as a per-terrain boolean, *directional* concealment, **smoke as a terrain type**, and a per-ammunition-family damage matrix — and `AutoCover` now reads a `TerrainListMask` instead of hard-coding forest. A distance threshold became a **roll rule shared by hitting, morale and being spotted** (with `// 2d6` comments left stranded over d100 parameters, and a deliberately degenerate always-succeed penetration roll). `SR_RefreshPositionEnnemi = 60` became a **belief model** — `Seen` versus `HasShot`, a 210/320/450-second decay, and difficulty expressed as what the AI is allowed to *learn* rather than as a damage multiplier. Influence maps arrived as first-class, tuned data (tactical 500 m cells, strategic 100 m, an isolated cell forgetting **eighty times faster** than a connected one, and a comment carrying the perf warning, the admitted design smell and the real 300×300 budget) — which sharpens §5.4's split rather than contradicting §5. NDF itself grew templates and **`* Metre` unit literals**, which is the direct fix for the millimetre/metre mix-up §7.1 catches in R.U.S.E.; `TTunableConstante` survived but gained a shouted alphabetical-order rule and was split in two. Deception came out **wholesale and left no residue** — pervasive turned out not to mean entangled. §12 states exactly what the readers could not decode, so the reliable parts are separable from the inferred ones. |

Not what the genre's reputation suggests: it is **Unity + HDRP + IL2CPP**, with **six in-house assemblies against ~90 bought ones**, and the split is exact — everything that renders, streams, serialises, transports or upscales was purchased; | [`nuclear_option.md`](nuclear_option.md) | **Vehicle physics — fixed wing, rotary, VTOL, ground and ships — read line by line, with §17 a reconstruction guide.** Nuclear Option ships **Mono**, so `Assembly-CSharp.dll` decompiles to near-original C# and the whole simulation can be read. It is also the exact counterweight to [`broken_arrow.md`](broken_arrow.md): same engine, same era, **26 third-party assemblies of which none render anything**, and §16 argues the buy/build line follows the *product* — Broken Arrow sells a battlefield so it bought rendering; this sells how the vehicles feel, and there is no package for that. The governing idea (§2) is that **the vehicle's geometry *is* its physics model**: parts carry an area, an airfoil and a transform, and every tick the flow is resolved **in each transform's own frame**. So pitching moment is the tailplane's force applied aft; **sideslip needs no term** because a fin is a part rolled 90° and the same `atan2` is its slip angle; flaps are a rewritten wing area; a listing ship is off-centre buoyancy. §4 is the one to read: **control surfaces produce a quaternion and nothing else** — `ControlSurfaceJob → SetLocalRotationJob → ReadTransformJob → AeroJob`, an explicit `JobHandle` chain — so control authority, its falloff with dynamic pressure, and elevator *stall* are emergent and none is coded. §7 is a genuine **blade-element rotor**: a flapping hinge integrated as β̈ = M/I against an asymmetric droop-stop spring, cyclic as the **projection of blade azimuth onto a tilted swashplate transform**, 4× azimuthal sub-stepping because a 280 rpm rotor turns 34° per tick, and **autorotation and torque reaction that are not implemented** — they fall out of `ω += (Q_engine + Q_aero − friction)·dt / I`. §8.4 solves the VTOL lift-fan thrust from a **moment balance about the current centre of mass**, every tick, in six lines. §9 is a hand-written raycast suspension with soft-ground sinkage and a **contact-patch stiction anchor** — the correct fix for parked vehicles creeping. §11 is the ships: **one rigidbody, N compartments as buoyancy probes**, `lerp(ρ_air, ρ_water, submerged)·g·V` for a smooth waterline, anisotropic quadratic drag (lateral ≫ longitudinal — that is the keel), flooding against an **exhaustible damage-control pool** that propagates to neighbours when spent, a hull that slows because its longitudinal drag lerps toward its lateral one, and **death by posture** (below the surface, heeled past 60°, bow up past 14°) rather than by hit points. **The sea is a flat plane and the wake is particles** — §11.3 and §11.7 are the honest negative findings, and the lesson that *a medium's fidelity should be set by what interacts with it*. §12 is the cost answer: hot fields as POD in native memory with the MonoBehaviour intact (CLAUDE.md's DOD/OOP split, arrived at independently), AI run in the job latency window, and **remote vehicles never enter the jobs at all**. §13 names the trade: **simulation depth and server authority trade against each other directly**. §18 ranks what transfers. **No talk, blog or paper exists**, and §19 says what was not established — including that per-vehicle authored values are unrecoverable because this build ships with script type trees stripped, so §17's numbers are physically derived rather than Shockfront's. |
| [`nuclear_option_combat.md`](nuclear_option_combat.md) | The combat half of [`nuclear_option.md`](nuclear_option.md) — **sensors, guidance and AI flight, and the one idea that ties them together: every counter-tactic in the game attacks a named term in a named equation, and you can point at both.** §1 is the radar: a **closed-form horizon test** (`sqrt(2Rh)` per end, summed) that rejects before the raycast; clutter as `targetRadius²·2 / radarAlt²`, **inverse-square in height above terrain**, subtracted *before* the detection floor; and signal as `maxRange/dist · RCS^0.25`, where the fourth root makes stealth valuable but never decisive. **§1.5 is notching, and it is one dot product** — `min(|dot(losDir, targetVelocity)|, 150) · dopplerFactor` applied as a *multiplier*, so flying abeam does not hide you, it removes your processing gain; you disappear only by stacking it with the clutter term, which is why the AI does both. The doppler response is linear rather than a sharp filter — a named, deliberate simplification. §2 is the missile: total-incidence lift/drag curves on a single body, a **predictive** rate limiter using the same `ω = 9.81g/V` as the aircraft FBW (so a fast missile turns badly and drag rises with incidence — that is what makes forcing it to manoeuvre a defence), and **a PID autopilot that is emphatically not proportional navigation**: the lead lives in the *seeker's* predicted aimpoint, so eight seekers share one autopilot and loft, terminal jink and datalink midcourse are all just aimpoint edits. §3 covers the seekers — ARH with a real datalink→active→terminal sequence, lock perseverance and a **fixed per-missile position bias generated at launch rather than per-frame noise**; and the IR flare decision as a single comparison whose terms are the entire IRCM playbook (**4:1 rear-aspect advantage**, sun glare scaled by the *sun's blue channel* so it blinds at noon and not at dusk, angular separation from flare drag). §5 has two lead solvers for two consumers and a firing cone scaled by apparent angular size. **§7 is the reason to read it**: the AI is not privileged — it writes the same `ControlInputs` struct the player's stick does and goes through the same fly-by-wire — so it must solve turn radius and energy for real. It does, in one line: `RotateTowards(heading, toTarget, 0.9 · (V/Vcorner)²)` — **rate-limit the demand, not the response**, with the square matching the way radius scales. Plus roll-before-pull, rudder banned above 20° off, idle-throttle rules expressed against corner speed, `sqrt(mass/maxWeight)` approach speed, and §7.5 where the AI computes the notch vector as a **double cross product** — the analytic solution for “minimise that dot product” — descends to 10 m for clutter, and releases chaff *only* once established in the notch. Difficulty is `randomError / skill` on the evasion geometry, so a weak AI flies badly rather than cheating. §8 ranks fourteen transferable ideas and two anti-patterns. |
| [`nuclear_option_command.md`](nuclear_option_command.md) | The third Nuclear Option note — **everything above and beside the single aircraft**, and the observation that ties it together: **there is one world model, one target-scoring function and one reservation mechanism, and every AI shares all three.** §1 is `TrackingInfo`, 90 lines that are the game's entire knowledge model: **fog of war is one timestamp and a four-second grace** (`GetPosition()` returns the truth if seen recently and the frozen memory otherwise, so every consumer inherits it for free), and **multi-unit deconfliction is two `sbyte`s** — `attackers` and `missileAttacks` sit in the denominator of every scoring function, so committing to a target makes it less attractive to everyone else. No blackboard, no assignment solver. §2 is `CombatAI.AnalyzeTarget`, called by fighters, SAM batteries and ships alike: hard rejections then multiplicative scoring, with a **minimum engagement altitude that scales with range** (terrain masking for the decision layer, agreeing with the radar horizon in the sensor layer) and `InterceptViability`, which projects the target's escape rate onto the weapon's top speed — the cure for AI that takes tail-chase shots it cannot win. §4's battery brain reserves with a lifetime (increment on plan, decrement on fire *or* cancel, re-validate between) and takes **planning time proportional to salvo size**. §5.1 is the best small piece of design in the codebase: `GetEvasionPoint()` is virtual and returns the *illuminating aircraft* for a semi-active missile, so the notch code from [`nuclear_option_combat.md`](nuclear_option_combat.md) automatically defends against the right object with no per-type logic. §6 is the helicopter: one controller covering hover→cruise by lerping the *error definition* rather than the gains, a **gun-run orbit in fifteen lines** (steer at the tangent, rotate toward the target in proportion to range error), and an autopilot that reads `GetVRSFactor()` and rotor RPM droop back out of the blade-element model and applies the real recovery techniques — the AI as a consumer of emergent physics, not a parallel model of it. §7 is ground vehicles, whose **driving logic runs inside a Burst job** with round-robin amortisation, and whose speed-for-corner rule is split across two systems that share no constants: navigation reports a corner angle *only once inside braking distance*, locomotion turns that angle into a speed limit. Plus a three-line **bulldoze timer** that breaks convoy deadlock, and a 2 m lateral steer offset so traffic drives on the right. §8's ships set **throttle to the cosine of the steering error** and synthesise a *virtual obstacle* for shore avoidance rather than adding a shore behaviour. §9 is the war: an excess-funds valve, **AI count scaled inversely with friendly players with an airframe reserve floor so bots never spend what humans need**, and a nuclear escalation ladder that is one monotonic scalar with two thresholds. §10 ranks eighteen transferable ideas; §11 lists what all three notes still leave unread. |
| [`nuclear_option_control.md`](nuclear_option_control.md) | The fourth Nuclear Option note — **every path from an input to a force**, human or AI, in the air, on the ground, on the water and on foot, plus the wind field that perturbs all of them. Written to close the gaps the other three left, and it lands on two observations. **One: there is exactly one control interface and everything writes to it.** `ControlInputs` is six floats; a stick, a mouse, an AI combat state, an autopilot and a taxi procedure all write it, then it goes through the same fly-by-wire and the same aerodynamics — so the AI cannot cheat, every flight-model improvement improves the AI for free, and a replicated remote player's inputs animate their control surfaces with no separate path. **Two: there is one contact idiom, reused four times** — cast a ray down, spring-damper on the hit normal, righting torque toward the surface, **subtract the surface's own velocity**, clamp tangential force to a friction circle. That is the landing gear, the ground-vehicle suspension, the hovercraft skirt **and the pilot's legs**, which is why an ejected pilot tumbles, gets blown over by a shockwave, can be picked up by a sling hook and stands correctly on a moving carrier deck with no special case. §1.1 is the detail I would steal first: **the throttle detects absolute versus relative hardware from the axis delta** (`delta < 0.5` → snap to a real lever; otherwise treat it as a direction and ramp at 1/s), so a HOTAS and a keyboard are both correct with no setting. Mouse flight moves a *stick* — a clamped, self-centring virtual deflection — not an aim point. Buttons poll per frame, axes sample per fixed step. | [`nuclear_option_audio.md`](nuclear_option_audio.md) | The narrowest Nuclear Option note and the one with the highest ideas-per-line: **how do you make a vehicle sound right at 600 m/s, and a battlefield right when the things making noise are ten kilometres away?** Three separate hard problems, solved with **no audio middleware whatsoever** — no FMOD, no Wwise, no Steam Audio, no reverb, no occlusion — in about 250 lines of stock Unity `AudioSource` and `AudioMixer`. §2 is that negative finding read against [`broken_arrow.md`](broken_arrow.md), which buys *two* audio packages for an easier problem. **§3, Doppler:** sources opt into a per-unit list, and the *camera state* toggles it — the aircraft you are flying or chasing gets `dopplerLevel = 0` and `spatialBlend = 0` (2D, centred), which kills the warble a chase camera's own motion creates *and* is the physically correct answer, since you do not experience Doppler from a source you travel with. Everything else runs at **0.6, not 1.0**, because full-strength Doppler at supersonic closure sounds like a broken tape. Then `JetNozzle` damps it further with one dot product — `max(1 − 2·camFacing, 0.01)`, near-zero within 60° of astern, exactly the geometry of every tail chase — and gets a **4:1 rear-to-front exhaust loudness** out of the same dot product. **§4, distance:** explosions register a *pending* sound on an expanding wavefront (`propagation += 340f * dt`, started at the cube-root fireball radius) and play when the sphere reaches the listener — with the low-pass cutoff set to **`22000 / travelTime`**, one divide that models atmospheric absorption and turns a delayed sound from a *late* one into a *distant* one. Camera shake fires at arrival, not detonation, so desynchronisation is impossible. **§5, supersonic:** the aircraft is **muted while you are outside its Mach cone** (`μ = asin(1/M)`), the boom fires once on entry with its source placed at the *emission point* walked back along the flight path, and the engine noise switching on is the same state transition — plus a guard so you do not boom yourself in chase view. §6 drives every engine from **two state variables** (pitch from RPM, volume mostly from power) and swaps *clips* rather than filtering for interior. §7 is start/loop/end gunfire with a spin-up pitch ramp. §8 is the cheap stuff that carries most of the quality: randomise clip, pitch, volume **and loop phase** for anything instanced; create sources lazily and destroy them; make the audio LOD the graphics LOD; and do G-LOC as a **master-bus low-pass closing to 250 Hz** so it affects sounds written later. §9 ranks seventeen transferable ideas and one honest ceiling. |
| [`nuclear_option_damage.md`](nuclear_option_damage.md) | The sixth Nuclear Option note, closing the last real hole: **the other five describe damage *arriving* as four numbers and never say who computes them.** §1 is a two-knob armour model — a **subtractive threshold** (what bounces off) and a **divisive tolerance** (how much what gets through hurts), which are different properties and are usually conflated; Plus the reminder that most effects are 20 particles and a dispatch costs more than the loop. §13.4 is Valve's own counter-example: CS2's headline effect is volumetric smoke, and they solved it with a **voxel volume rather than more particles** — when a particle system is asked for a dense participating medium, the fix is a different representation. §13.5 lists the three fill-rate fixes that are *rendering*-side and orthogonal to all of it (half-res off-screen with depth-aware upsample, tighter quads, compute-binned tiles), all of which are "do less work" while moving the sim is "do the same work elsewhere". ## Where the XCOM 2 material came from

Unlike From Dust, none of this is guesswork from a disassembly. UE3 ships its materials as
serialised node graphs inside the package, so the graph *is* recoverable — the SDK's packages
are uncompressed and the name, import and export tables read straight out:

```
XCOM 2 SDK/XComGame/Content/XCOM_2/Packages/UI/UI_3D.upk
  -> Tile.MovementBorder                      (UE3 package version 845, licensee 105)
```

which is the material the game is told to use, in
`XCOM 2 SDK/XComGame/Config/XComEngine.ini`:

```
MovementGridMaterialName=UI_3D.Tile.MovementBorder
MovementGridDotsMaterialName=UI_3D.Tile.MovementTile
```

Every constant, connection and material flag in the HLSL is transcribed from the
`MaterialExpression` objects under that material. The two `MaterialExpressionCustom` bodies are
literal — they are the only hand-written HLSL that ships inside a UE3 material, and they are
quoted exactly:

```hlsl
return 1.0-saturate((WorldPos.z - (FadeHeight-FadeDistance))/ FadeDistance);   // "Height Fading"
```

Node defaults that the package leaves unserialised were read from the SDK's own UnrealScript,
`Development/SrcOrig/Engine/Classes/MaterialExpression*.uc` — which matters more than it sounds:
`MaterialExpressionConstantScale` defaults its `Scale` to **0.5**, not 1.

Two more sources fill in the parts the material does not own:

- `XComGame/Config/XComGame.ini`, `[XComGame.XComMovementGridComponent]` — the ribbon's width,
  height offset, length factor and UV tiling distance. These shape the mesh, and the material's
  UVs only mean anything alongside them.
- `XComEngineMaterials.upk` → `MovementGrid_Material` — the engine-default fallback. A different,
  older take on the same idea, and worth reading against the shipping one: it drives its emissive
  from a cursor-proximity glow and reverses the depth comparison. Its differences are noted inline
  in the HLSL.

## What is *not* recovered

`MovementBorder_Line`, the 64×64 BC5 two-channel texture holding the line's cross-section
profiles. The port reconstructs both channels analytically in
[`../assets/shaders/ribbon.fs.glsl`](../assets/shaders/ribbon.fs.glsl) rather than shipping the
game's asset. That is the single deviation, and it is a deviation of asset, not of maths.
