# Study

Notes on how the games this prototype borrows from draw what they draw, written
for understanding the system rather than for dropping into an engine.

The governing idea (§2) is that **the vehicle's geometry *is* its physics model**: parts carry an area, an airfoil and a transform, and every tick the flow is resolved **in each transform's own frame**. So pitching moment is the tailplane's force applied aft; **sideslip needs no term** because a fin is a part rolled 90° and the same `atan2` is its slip angle; flaps are a rewritten wing area; a listing ship is off-centre buoyancy. §4 is the one to read: **control surfaces produce a quaternion and nothing else** — `ControlSurfaceJob → SetLocalRotationJob → ReadTransformJob → AeroJob`, an turns 34° per tick, and **autorotation and torque reaction that are not implemented** — they fall out of `ω += (Q_engine + Q_aero − friction)·dt / I`. §8.4 solves the VTOL lift-fan thrust from a **moment balance about the current centre of mass**, every tick, in six lines. §9 is a hand-written raycast suspension with soft-ground sinkage and a **contact-patch stiction anchor** — the correct fix for parked vehicles creeping. §11 is the ships: **one rigidbody, N compartments as buoyancy probes**, `lerp(ρ_air, ρ_water, submerged)·g·V` for a smooth waterline, anisotropic quadratic drag (lateral ≫ longitudinal — that is the keel), flooding against an **exhaustible damage-control pool** that propagates to neighbours when spent, a hull that slows because its longitudinal drag lerps toward its lateral one, and **death by posture** (below the surface, heeled past 60°, bow up past 14°) rather than by hit points. **The sea is a flat plane and the wake is particles** — §11.3 and §11.7 are the honest negative findings, and the lesson that *a medium's fidelity should be set by what interacts with it*. §12 is the cost answer: hot fields as POD in native memory with the depth and server authority trade against each other directly**. §18 ranks what transfers. **No talk, blog or paper exists**, and §19 says what was not established — including that per-vehicle authored values are unrecoverable because this build ships with script type trees stripped, so §17's numbers are physically derived rather than Shockfront's. |
| [`nuclear_option_combat.md`](games/flight/nuclear_option/nuclear_option_combat.md) | The combat half of [`nuclear_option.md`](games/flight/nuclear_option/nuclear_option.md) — **sensors, guidance and AI flight, and the one idea that ties them together: every counter-tactic in the game attacks a named term in a named equation, and you can point at both.** §1 is the radar: a **closed-form horizon test** (`sqrt(2Rh)` per end, summed) that rejects before the raycast; clutter as `targetRadius²·2 / radarAlt²`, **inverse-square in height above terrain**, subtracted *before* the detection floor; and signal as `maxRange/dist · RCS^0.25`, where the fourth root makes stealth valuable but never decisive. **§1.5 is notching, and it is one dot product** — `min(|dot(losDir, targetVelocity)|, 150) · dopplerFactor` applied as a *multiplier*, so flying abeam does not hide you, it removes your processing gain; you disappear only by stacking it with the clutter term, which is why the AI does both. Plus roll-before-pull, rudder banned above 20° off, idle-throttle rules expressed against corner speed, `sqrt(mass/maxWeight)` approach speed, and §7.5 where the AI computes the notch vector as a **double cross product** — the analytic solution for “minimise that dot product” — descends to 10 m for clutter, and releases chaff *only* once established in the notch. Difficulty is `randomError / skill` on the evasion geometry, so a weak AI flies badly rather than cheating. §8 ranks fourteen transferable ideas and two anti-patterns. |
| [`nuclear_option_command.md`](games/flight/nuclear_option/nuclear_option_command.md) | The third Nuclear Option note — **everything above and beside the single aircraft**, and the observation that ties it together: **there is one world model, one target-scoring function and one reservation mechanism, and every AI shares all three.** §1 is `TrackingInfo`, 90 lines that are the game's entire knowledge model: **fog of war is one timestamp and a four-second grace** (`GetPosition()` returns the truth if seen recently and the frozen memory otherwise, so every consumer inherits it for free), and **multi-unit deconfliction is two `sbyte`s** — `attackers` and `missileAttacks` sit in the denominator of every scoring function, so committing to a target makes it less attractive to everyone else. | [`nuclear_option_control.md`](games/flight/nuclear_option/nuclear_option_control.md) | The fourth Nuclear Option note — **every path from an input to a force**, human or AI, in the air, on the ground, on the water and on foot, plus the wind field that perturbs all of them. Written to close the gaps the other three left, and it lands on two observations. **One: there is exactly one control interface and everything writes to it.** `ControlInputs` is six floats; a stick, a mouse, an AI combat state, an autopilot and a taxi procedure all write it, then it goes through the same fly-by-wire and the same aerodynamics — so the AI cannot cheat, every flight-model improvement improves the AI for free, and a replicated remote player's inputs animate their control surfaces with no separate path. **Two: there is one contact idiom, reused four times** — cast a ray down, spring-damper on the hit normal, righting torque toward the surface, **subtract the surface's own velocity**, clamp tangential force to a friction circle. | [`nuclear_option_audio.md`](games/flight/nuclear_option/nuclear_option_audio.md) | The narrowest Nuclear Option note and the one with the highest ideas-per-line: **how do you make a vehicle sound right at 600 m/s, and a battlefield right when the things making noise are ten kilometres away?** Three separate hard problems, solved with **no audio middleware whatsoever** — no FMOD, no Wwise, no Steam Audio, no reverb, no occlusion — in about 250 lines of stock Unity `AudioSource` and `AudioMixer`. §2 is that negative finding read against [`broken_arrow.md`](games/flight/broken_arrow/broken_arrow.md), which buys *two* audio packages for an easier problem. **§3, Doppler:** sources opt into a per-unit list, and the *camera state* toggles it — the aircraft you are flying or chasing gets `dopplerLevel = 0` and `spatialBlend = 0` (2D, centred), which kills the warble a chase camera's own motion creates *and* is the physically correct answer, since you do not experience Doppler from a source you travel with. Everything else runs at **0.6, not 1.0**, because full-strength Doppler at supersonic closure sounds like a broken tape. Then `JetNozzle` damps it further with one dot product — `max(1 − 2·camFacing, 0.01)`, near-zero within 60° of astern, exactly the geometry of every tail chase — and gets a **4:1 rear-to-front exhaust loudness** out of the same dot product. **§4, distance:** explosions register a *pending* sound on an expanding wavefront (`propagation += 340f * dt`, started at the cube-root fireball radius) and play when the sphere reaches the listener — with the low-pass cutoff set to **`22000 / travelTime`**, one divide that models atmospheric absorption and turns a delayed sound from a *late* one into a *distant* one. | [`broken_arrow_aircraft.md`](games/flight/broken_arrow/broken_arrow_aircraft.md) | **How does an aircraft reach a waypoint accurately enough to bomb with, and what happens when the order changes on short final?** The counterpart to [`nuclear_option_command.md`](games/flight/nuclear_option/nuclear_option_command.md) §6–8, and the two answer it at **opposite poles for a structural reason**. | [`broken_arrow_squads.md`](games/flight/broken_arrow/broken_arrow_squads.md) | **How do you run infantry at RTS scale without simulating nine men?** Read from the same retail Il2CppDumper dump as the aircraft note, and the answer is a **three-tier entity model** where almost every property falls out of *where a decision was placed*. **The squad is the unit** — one path, one `HealthComponent`, one `BoxCollider`, one set of eyes, one avoidance agent, one FMOD instance — and every battle system touches it with **the identical code that drives a tank**. **The soldier is a body**: a real entity, but with no health, no collider, no path, no vision and no target; its entire combat state is `IsAiming` and which of two guns is in its hands. `shadowTap`); **every other type degrades from it** — a cube face *is* a 90° spot and an un-LTC'd rect light *is* a wide-apex spot, so doing the point first pays the six-face tax before the flat case is known to work; it has the tightest culling volume; and it is the type whose **motion is legible**, since a travelling cone edge reads where a travelling glow does not. Shadows move to second because leaking through walls is a day-one problem for a *movable* light in a cutaway interior, not a scale problem — but scoped to **one map**, since a slot allocator, a static/dynamic policy and a re-render budget designed at N=1 are three guesses about requirements P3 is what actually produces. **§7.2 is the section that saves the most time**, and it exists because `shadow.glsl`'s own header settles a question for the sun that reads at a glance as settling it for both: *"THE TEXTBOOK FORMULA DOES NOT APPLY: (receiver − blocker) / blocker comes from similar triangles under a PERSPECTIVE light"* — a spot **is** perspective, so for it the textbook formula is the correct one and the sun's `2 × distance × tan(angular radius)` is the wrong one. Three more invert with it: perspective depth is **non-linear**, so the blocker search's world-distance arithmetic stops meaning anything; the **world size of a texel varies across the map**, so the single `uShadowScales.y` that both the normal-offset bias and the penumbra floor depend on gives acne near the light and peter-panning far from it, presenting as "the bias needs tuning" and unfixable by tuning; and **the texel-snap trick is simply unavailable**, because snapping works by translating an orthographic projection a whole texel and a *rotating* spot has no increment to snap to — so a moving spot's edge will shimmer and the only answers are resolution, filter width and acceptance. What *does* transfer is most of the file's hard-won content and none of its geometry: `shadowTap`'s compare-then-interpolate, the rotated Poisson disc and the caster material. **§5.5 is what "dynamic and movable" costs on its own**: the whole light buffer re-uploads every frame (dirty-tracking is a second description of the truth that can disagree with the first — the decal path's decision, for the decal path's reason); **a moving light's shadow map cannot be cached, so the limit on shadowed dynamic lights is a scene-draw budget rather than a shading one**, which is the opposite of where intuition points; and local lights return a **scalar** shadow while the sun keeps its colour, because a per-light transmission plane doubles shadow cost for an effect that only reads through glass. §6 settles ownership by precedent rather than argument — the light set is **world-scoped on `RenderScene`** exactly as `RenderScene.hpp` argues for the probes, the froxel grid is **view-scoped on `ScenePipeline`**, tuning sits with `BloomTuning` — and notes the leak to avoid: `SunLight.hpp` includes `raylib.h` and a lighting type added *after* the RHI landed has no excuse to. §7 is the decisions that get made wrong if not made here: **log-distributed depth slices** (linear puts every froxel where nothing is and lumps the whole foreground into slice zero, under a camera whose subject is always close), the loop in an include from the first line rather than copied later, the transparent pass looping lights too, a max-per-froxel cap whose overflow **presents as flicker** and therefore needs logging and a panel row — and the one that inverts a rule: **local lights should be ON in a probe capture, unlike probes themselves**, because neither reason for `uProbeParams.x = 0` (a texture that is its own attachment; error compounding per sweep) applies to a light, and switching them off would make reflections systematically darker than the surfaces beside them. §9 rejects tiled-2D culling (tiles collapse wherever one spans a near object and a far one — every unit silhouetted against the street), **per-object light lists** (structurally impossible here, not merely crude: the statics are one mesh **per storey**, so four lights would serve an entire floor), shadows-first, and building anything before RHI parity — since §1 of `MIGRATION.md` shows the raylib path *cannot* be driven by a `std140` block, so a light SSBO is precisely the input it can never take. **§10's first open question is the one that decides the shape of the whole feature**: a tactics board wants a *dozen* lights, not a thousand, and at a dozen the P1 naive loop is not a stepping stone but the finished feature — so the cluster grid waits for a measurement or a content plan, and building it because the technique is *called* clustered forward is the exact mistake CLAUDE.md's measurement rule exists to prevent. The rest: whether the spot wants a **cookie** (a gobo, mullions, an IES profile — one sample inside a loop that already computed the light-space position for the shadow, so nearly free once P2 exists, and most of what makes a spot read as a *fixture* rather than a cone), whether transient effects become lights at all (a one-frame muzzle flash is a light that pops, and `particle_architecture.md` reserves no attribute for it), whether a light is an authored asset, the LTC tables as shipped data, and how any of it interacts with the sun bake. |

## Where the XCOM 2 material came from

Unlike From Dust, none of this is guesswork from a disassembly. UE3 ships its materials as
serialised node graphs inside the package, so the graph *is* recoverable — the SDK's packages
are uncompressed and the name, import and export tables read straight out:

```
XCOM 2 SDK/XComGame/Content/XCOM_2/Packages/UI/UI_3D.upk
  -> Tile.MovementBorder                      (UE3 package version 845, licensee 105)
  -> CursorSet.CursorRibbon                   (same package)
```

which are the materials the game is told to use — the boundary in
`XCOM 2 SDK/XComGame/Config/XComEngine.ini`:

```
MovementGridMaterialName=UI_3D.Tile.MovementBorder
MovementGridDotsMaterialName=UI_3D.Tile.MovementTile
```

and the path line in `XComGame/Config/XComGameCore.ini`, `[XComGame.XComPathingPawn]`:

```
PathMaterialNormalName=UI_3D.CursorSet.CursorRibbon
PathMaterialDashingName=UI_3D.CursorSet.CursorRibbon_Dashing
```

The mechanical way to get either, rather than by hand — the SDK's own commandlet dumps a base
`Material`'s whole expression graph, not just the parameter overrides on its instances, which is
the step `tools/xcom2/xcom_materials.ps1` skips because it only ever needed
`MaterialInstanceConstant`:

```
cd "XCOM 2 SDK/Binaries/Win64"
XComGame.com batchexport UI_3D Material   T3D <out>     # 50 node graphs
XComGame.com batchexport UI_3D Texture2D  T3D <out>     # formats, address modes, source .psd
XComGame.com batchexport UI_3D Texture2D  TGA <out>     # the pixels
```

Every constant, connection and material flag in the HLSL is transcribed from the
`MaterialExpression` objects under that material. MovementBorder's two `MaterialExpressionCustom`
bodies are literal — they are the only hand-written HLSL that ships inside a UE3 material, and
they are quoted exactly (CursorRibbon has no Custom node at all):

```hlsl
return 1.0-saturate((WorldPos.z - (FadeHeight-FadeDistance))/ FadeDistance);   // "Height Fading"
```

Node defaults that the package leaves unserialised were read from the SDK's own UnrealScript,
`Development/SrcOrig/Engine/Classes/MaterialExpression*.uc` — which matters more than it sounds:
`MaterialExpressionConstantScale` defaults its `Scale` to **0.5**, not 1.

Two more sources fill in the parts the material does not own:

- `XComGame/Config/XComGame.ini`, `[XComGame.XComMovementGridComponent]` — the ribbon's width,
  height offset, length factor and UV tiling distance. These shape the mesh, and the material's
  UVs only mean anything alongside them.
- `XComEngineMaterials.upk` → `MovementGrid_Material` — the engine-default fallback. A different,
  older take on the same idea, and worth reading against the shipping one: it drives its emissive
  from a cursor-proximity glow and reverses the depth comparison. Its differences are noted inline
  in the HLSL.

For the path line the same two sources apply, in different files:

- `XComPathingPawn.uc` and `XComRenderablePathComponent.uc` — `fRibbonWidth`, `iPathLengthOffset`
  and `fEmitterTimeStep`, and the material swap between the teal and amber instances. Read the
  `defaultproperties`, not the ini: unlike the movement grid, these are not configurable.
- `XComPrecomputedPath.uc` — the grenade and rocket arc, which is the *same component and the same
  material* retuned to a 1.25-wide ribbon. Worth knowing before treating the path line as
  movement-specific.

## What is *not* recovered

Both profile textures **are** now recovered exactly, which corrects what this section said
before. `MovementBorder_Line` was written off as unreadable because it is BC5; the SDK's exporter
decompresses it on the way out (`batchexport UI_3D Texture2D TGA`), and `CursorTrail_MSK` ships
uncompressed anyway. Both are tabulated in their respective HLSL files, and they turn out to carry
the *same* shoulder curve on opposite axes — an S-curve that is not a smoothstep, so the 17 values
are worth copying rather than fitting.

That makes the port's analytic `lineProfile` in
[`../assets/shaders/ribbon.fs.glsl`](../src/cromwell/assets/shaders/ribbon.fs.glsl) checkable for the first
time, and it differs in three measurable ways — the section is about **half XCOM's width**, the
dash period is one per tile against two, and the dash gaps go to zero against 0.102. The first is
a deliberate look choice the shader comment already names; none of the three is settled, and the
numbers are in the study for whoever decides.

What is missing instead is the path line's **vertex colour ramp**: the ribbon mesh is built by native
code with no C++ in the SDK, so how alpha is distributed along the path is not readable. The
material bounds it — per-vertex, inverted, spanning `[0.5, 1.0]` of the profile — and that is
enough to port, but the ramp's exact shape is inferred rather than transcribed.
